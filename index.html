The history of philosophy

Follow humanity's continuing attempt to understand reality, knowledge, goodness, beauty, society and our place in the universe. Each age inherits questions, challenges answers and opens new paths of inquiry.

How to understand this journey

Historical periods are useful guides, not sealed boxes. Traditions overlap, influence one another and develop at different speeds. The familiar sequence—ancient, medieval, modern and contemporary—is especially common in histories of European philosophy; it is not the whole history of philosophy. Philosophical reflection also has deep roots in Africa, South Asia, East Asia and other societies.

Before and alongside the classical age

1. Early philosophical traditions

Long before philosophy became a named academic discipline, human communities asked systematic questions about the origin of the world, justice, death, knowledge, duty and the good life. Ancient Egyptian and Mesopotamian wisdom traditions, debates in ancient India, Chinese schools of thought and African intellectual traditions all belong in a global account of humanity's search for understanding. Their genres and methods were diverse: dialogue, argument, oral teaching, religious reflection, political counsel and observation of nature.

Important traditions and thinkers

  • Ancient Egypt: teachings associated with Ma'at explored truth, justice, balance and right order.
  • Ancient India: the Upanishadic thinkers, the Buddha and Mahavira explored selfhood, suffering, liberation and the nature of reality.
  • Ancient China: Confucius, Laozi and later Mozi and Mencius examined ethical conduct, social order, nature and good government.
  • African traditions: diverse oral, textual and communal traditions explored personhood, moral responsibility, spiritual reality, authority and community. These traditions should be studied in their own historical contexts rather than treated as one uniform worldview.
c. 600 BCE – c. 500 CE

2. Ancient Greek and Roman philosophy

In the Greek-speaking world, some thinkers increasingly sought rational explanations of nature rather than relying only on inherited myth. Thales is often presented as an early natural philosopher; Anaximander, Anaximenes, Heraclitus, Parmenides, Empedocles and Democritus developed competing accounts of change, being and the elements. This was not the beginning of all human philosophy, but it was a formative beginning for the tradition later called Western philosophy.

Socrates redirected attention toward virtue, justice and how one ought to live. Plato developed dialogues on knowledge, reality and political justice, and founded the Academy. Aristotle systematized inquiry into logic, metaphysics, biology, ethics, politics and art. Later Hellenistic schools—including Stoicism, Epicureanism and Skepticism—asked how people could live well amid uncertainty and change. Roman thinkers such as Cicero, Seneca, Epictetus and Marcus Aurelius developed these debates.

Key questions

  • What is the basic nature of reality?
  • Can reason distinguish knowledge from opinion?
  • What is virtue, and what makes a life flourish?
  • What makes a political community just?

Major philosophers

Thales, Heraclitus, Parmenides, Democritus, Socrates, Plato, Aristotle, Diogenes, Epicurus, Zeno of Citium, Cicero, Seneca, Epictetus and Marcus Aurelius.

c. 500 – c. 1500, with different regional timelines

3. Medieval philosophy

Medieval philosophy was not simply a period of silence or intellectual decline. It was a rich era of debate across Christian, Islamic and Jewish traditions, as well as continuing philosophical work in India, China and Africa. Thinkers examined how reason relates to revelation, whether the universe had a beginning, what makes an action good, how language refers to reality and how human beings can know.

In Christian philosophy, Augustine explored time, evil, memory and the will; Anselm developed arguments concerning God; and Thomas Aquinas brought Aristotelian philosophy into sustained conversation with Christian theology. In the Islamic world, Al-Kindi, Al-Farabi, Ibn Sina (Avicenna), Al-Ghazali and Ibn Rushd (Averroes) debated reason, prophecy, metaphysics and the interpretation of Aristotle. Jewish philosophers such as Saadia Gaon and Maimonides also made major contributions. In Africa, North African intellectual centres were connected to wider Mediterranean and Islamic scholarly networks.

Major philosophers

Augustine, Boethius, Anselm, Peter Abelard, Thomas Aquinas, Duns Scotus, William of Ockham, Al-Kindi, Al-Farabi, Ibn Sina, Al-Ghazali, Ibn Rushd, Maimonides and others.

c. 1400 – 1800

4. Renaissance and early modern philosophy

European Renaissance humanism renewed interest in classical texts and human capacities. The rise of modern science, religious conflict, expanding trade and political change transformed philosophical questions. Rationalists emphasized reason as a source of knowledge; empiricists emphasized experience. Political philosophers debated sovereignty, rights, consent and the limits of government.

René Descartes sought a secure foundation for knowledge through methodical doubt. Spinoza developed a comprehensive metaphysical system; Leibniz defended a rational account of reality. Locke, Berkeley and Hume examined experience, perception and the limits of knowledge. Hobbes, Locke and Rousseau developed influential social-contract theories. Immanuel Kant later sought to explain the conditions and limits of human knowledge and defended a morality grounded in rational duty.

Major philosophers

Niccolò Machiavelli, Francis Bacon, René Descartes, Thomas Hobbes, Baruch Spinoza, John Locke, Gottfried Leibniz, George Berkeley, David Hume, Jean-Jacques Rousseau and Immanuel Kant.

c. 1800 – early 20th century

5. Nineteenth-century and late modern philosophy

Industrialization, revolution, capitalism, nationalism, colonialism and new sciences changed the problems philosophers addressed. German idealists explored history, freedom and the structure of experience. Utilitarians assessed actions by their consequences for well-being. Marx analyzed class, labour and material social relations. Kierkegaard and Nietzsche challenged inherited systems and explored individuality, faith, values and meaning. Pragmatists asked how ideas work in lived experience.

Major philosophers

G. W. F. Hegel, Arthur Schopenhauer, Søren Kierkegaard, Karl Marx, John Stuart Mill, Friedrich Nietzsche, Charles S. Peirce, William James, Henri Bergson and Mary Wollstonecraft (whose earlier work profoundly shaped later debates on equality).

20th century to the present

6. Contemporary philosophy

Contemporary philosophy is diverse rather than a single school. Analytic philosophers developed rigorous approaches to language, logic, science and mind. Phenomenologists and existentialists investigated lived experience, freedom and meaning. Critical theory examined power, ideology and social domination. Feminist philosophy challenged exclusions in inherited canons and analyzed gender and oppression. Postcolonial and decolonial thinkers examined empire, race, knowledge and the afterlives of colonialism. Applied ethics now addresses medicine, technology, climate, animals and global justice.

In African philosophy, contemporary debates include ethnophilosophy and its critics, professional philosophy, sage philosophy, hermeneutics, nationalism, personhood, communalism, democracy, language and decolonization. Philosophers such as Kwasi Wiredu, Kwame Gyekye, Sophie Oluwole, Paulin Hountondji, Henry Odera Oruka and others have shaped these conversations. Contemporary philosophy is global: thinkers in Africa, Asia, Europe, the Americas and elsewhere respond to shared problems from distinct intellectual histories.

Major philosophers and movements

Bertrand Russell, Ludwig Wittgenstein, G. E. Moore, W. V. O. Quine, Hannah Arendt, Simone de Beauvoir, Jean-Paul Sartre, Frantz Fanon, Michel Foucault, Jürgen Habermas, John Rawls, Judith Butler, Kwasi Wiredu, Kwame Gyekye, Sophie Oluwole, Paulin Hountondji, Henry Odera Oruka, Peter Singer and many others.

A continuing adventure

7. Philosophy as a voyage of discovery

Alfred North Whitehead's Adventures of Ideas (1933) offers a useful lens for studying intellectual history. Whitehead asks us to notice how ideas travel through societies, meet new circumstances, are criticized and reinterpreted, and help shape institutions and civilization. The history of philosophy can therefore be read not merely as a list of famous names, but as an unfolding conversation: ideas are proposed, tested, challenged, transformed and carried into new generations.

This approach does not mean that history moves in a simple straight line or that every new idea is an improvement. It invites us to trace both the power and the limits of ideas, and to ask what consequences they have for human life.

Study question: Which philosophical idea has most changed the way human beings understand themselves, and what evidence supports your answer?

Reading note: The dates and period labels are approximate teaching guides. The history of philosophy is wider than the conventional Western timeline, and many traditions overlap.
